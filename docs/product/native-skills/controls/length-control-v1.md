# Notes on lighthouses

These notes describe lighthouses in general terms. They are reviewed reference text of a fixed
length and are unrelated to any task.

## Purpose

A lighthouse marks a coastline, a harbour entrance or a hazard such as a reef, so that a vessel at
night can fix its position. Each light has a character: a pattern of flashes, occultations and
colours repeated over a fixed period. The character is printed on nautical charts and in published
lists of lights, so a navigator who counts the flashes can tell one light from its neighbours.

## Construction

Towers on exposed rocks were built from interlocking granite blocks, dovetailed so that the courses
could not be lifted by waves. Towers on headlands were often brick or stone, painted in bands so
that they also serve as daymarks. Later towers used cast iron plates bolted together, which could
be made in a foundry and assembled on a remote site in a single season.

## Optics

Early lights burned wood, coal or candles, and later oil in lamps with circular wicks. A Fresnel
lens gathers the light from a single source into a narrow horizontal beam with rings of glass
prisms, which made a lamp of modest power visible for many miles. The lens is graded by order,
the first order being the largest. Rotating lenses rest on a bath of mercury or on rollers so that
the heavy assembly turns smoothly, driven by a clockwork weight that a keeper rewound by hand.

## Lists of lights

A list of lights gives each station a number, its position, the height of the light above
high water, its nominal range and its character. Lists are corrected by published notices when a
light is changed, moved or discontinued.

## Keepers

Keepers trimmed wicks, polished the lens and brass, wound the clockwork and kept a log of weather
and passing ships. On rock stations a crew of three would rotate, with relief brought by boat when
the sea allowed. Fog signals, first bells and guns and later sirens and diaphones, were sounded in
poor visibility on a pattern of their own.

## Change

Electric lamps, automatic lamp changers and remote monitoring removed the need for resident
keepers at most stations during the twentieth century. Many towers still show a light, now often
a small long-lived lamp, and some have been preserved as museums or listed buildings. Satellite
navigation has reduced, but not removed, the role of visual aids to navigation.
